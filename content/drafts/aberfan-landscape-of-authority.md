The Aberfan coal-tip disaster killed 144 people, including 116 children, on 21 October 1966. Glamorgan Archives records that the subsequent tribunal held the National Coal Board responsible. Its collections connect the hillside to the institutions managing the material accumulated above the community.

The archive holds transcripts from all 76 tribunal sitting days alongside witness statements, weather reports, correspondence and plans. Together they allow the disaster to be examined as both a physical failure and a history of decisions about material stored above a community.

## The work inside the landscape

Calling an event a landslide describes motion. It does not finish the explanation of how unstable material came to be there, who understood its condition, or who could require a change. The language of nature can be descriptively useful and politically incomplete at the same time.

Ground conditions and accumulated material explain the mechanism. Siting, monitoring and continued use explain how that mechanism became a risk to the community. Responsibility cannot be examined by excluding either part.

This is one reason maps matter. They can connect an administrative record to the space it describes: a tip, a settlement, routes of movement, a place where children learned. A map cannot supply the missing perspective of a resident, but it can prevent an institutional account from floating free of the people affected by its decisions.

## What the archive makes visible

Glamorgan Archives records that 144 people died in the disaster, including 116 children. It dates the appointment of the tribunal to 26 October 1966 and identifies the National Coal Board as the institution held responsible by its findings. The same archive describes a collection containing transcripts from all 76 sitting days, witness statements, meteorological reports, correspondence and plans.

Weather reports, plans and testimony answer different questions. Reading them together connects the material conditions to the knowledge and authority available to act on them.

The archive also records that comprehensive information about the board's tips was assembled following the tribunal's recommendation. This is a concrete administrative consequence: an inventory was needed before the condition of an entire set of sites could be examined systematically.

There is a difference between possessing knowledge somewhere and organizing it so someone has to act on it. An isolated plan may be accurate without entering a safety decision. A comprehensive inventory can expose omissions, but only if responsibility for maintaining and using it is clear. Aberfan's documentary aftermath makes that distinction visible without requiring the assertion that paperwork, by itself, makes a landscape safe.

## After the finding

The tribunal's findings established responsibility within an inquiry. Implementation and the community's experience afterward require their own records.

The next questions concern implementation and remembrance. What was changed? Who paid? What did the community have to ask for again? Those questions need further records rather than a confident assertion that lessons were learned.

The hillside was the product of decisions made before it moved. The tribunal made responsibility traceable; the resulting inventory of tips extended the inquiry beyond Aberfan. Effective protection still depended on what institutions did with that knowledge.
