An explosion at Port Chicago, California, killed 320 people on 17 July 1944. Less than a month later, survivors were assigned to resume loading ammunition at another facility. Some refused, and prosecutions followed, according to the National Park Service.

The NPS history describes a segregated naval system and inadequate training exposed in the proceedings. The order to resume loading thus followed an unresolved question about safety: what had changed since the explosion for the men being sent back to handle ammunition?

### Duty does not describe the distribution of risk

Military language can present an order as the beginning of a story. For the person receiving it, however, the story may begin with training, equipment, previous warnings or a colleague who did not survive. An investigation that starts only at the moment of refusal can make these earlier conditions difficult to see.

That is why a record of discipline should be read beside a record of working conditions. A charge explains what an authority alleged. It does not automatically establish why someone acted, what alternatives were available, or whether the work was organized safely. Nor does criticism of the institution require pretending to know a sailor's private reasoning without testimony.

When an institution assigns dangerous work along racial lines, the task, the ability to challenge it and the penalties for refusal belong in the same account.

### An order after an explosion

The NPS account describes survivors being assigned to resume loading at another nearby facility less than a month after the disaster. It also records that the ensuing proceedings exposed inadequate training for the dangerous work. This sequence connects the return-to-work question with the conditions preceding the refusal.

The move to another facility was a change of location. It did not, by itself, establish that the sailors had received the training or assurances needed to regard the task differently. Asking only whether they obeyed removes that question from the record before it can be examined. Asking about safety does not eliminate the military context; it makes the context include the organization of the work.

A later milestone must also remain visible. NPS records the official exoneration on 17 July 2024 of sailors court-martialed following the refusals. That decision belongs to the history of official judgment, eight decades after the explosion. It should not be described as though it occurred during the original proceedings or restored the time and opportunities lost to them.

Loading had to resume within weeks; official exoneration came eight decades later. The different timescales connect the immediate demands made of survivors to the long history of institutional judgment.

### The memorial and the worksite

A memorial names a loss. A work history asks how people were brought into the conditions that produced it. These are complementary acts of remembrance. To keep only the memorial is to risk making death appear detached from decisions; to keep only the institutional lesson is to reduce people to evidence for an argument.

The prosecutions began with the refusal to resume work. The work history began earlier, with training, segregation and the conditions of loading. Reading the records together preserves what an account beginning only with disobedience would miss.
