Waste oil spread to suppress dust on Times Beach's roads carried a danger residents could not identify from its immediate use. Investigators later found dioxin contamination. A major flood covered the Missouri town in December 1982, and federal agencies recommended permanent relocation.

The retrospective reports that more than 2,000 residents left. Contamination, flooding, relocation and disposal followed distinct stages. The flood brought a new emergency to an existing contamination problem; it did not create that problem or the Superfund law enacted in 1980.

## A road can distribute more than traffic

Dust control is an ordinary municipal problem. A substance spread for that purpose can become difficult to recognize as a hazard precisely because its immediate effect is useful. The road looks better while the history of the material applied to it remains elsewhere.

This makes provenance a practical environmental question. Where did the oil come from? What did its supplier know about its contents? Which records connected the source material to the places where it was used? A contaminant's movement can depend on a commercial or maintenance chain that residents never see.

The flood is a dated event in a longer contamination history. It cannot serve as the single explanation for the material's arrival, its recognition and the decisions made afterward.

EPA supplies an institutional chronology. Local records and residents' accounts are needed to explain how a technical relocation decision became the loss of a community.

Removing material, restricting land use and qualifying a site for a new purpose are different cleanup outcomes. None can establish that the earlier town was restored.

## Several clocks in one disaster

EPA's retrospective separates a history of waste-oil disposal from the flood and relocation decisions. Superfund legislation existed in 1980, before the 1982 flood. The flood did not create that law. Mixing those dates would turn a complicated response into an attractive but inaccurate origin story.

The contamination, its recognition, the decision that residents should not return, and the later cleanup each followed a different timetable. Their order matters because people made decisions using the knowledge available at each stage. A later account can identify a contaminated mixture without establishing that every earlier recipient understood its composition. Equally, an emergency evacuation does not describe the whole process of acquiring properties and managing waste. Preserving these separate clocks explains how an environmental incident became a long public project, rather than ending when the immediate water receded.

The floodwater receded. The contaminated material still required decisions about return, property acquisition and disposal. Times Beach became a continuing public project because the end of the flood did not mark the end of the hazard.
