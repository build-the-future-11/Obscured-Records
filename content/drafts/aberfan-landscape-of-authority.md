A hill can look like part of the background. A coal tip is also a record of work: material moved, accumulated and left in a particular relationship to the land beneath it and the people below.

The coal-tip disaster at Aberfan occurred on 21 October 1966. The National Archives catalogue identifies official correspondence about the catastrophe, the position of National Coal Board chairman Lord Robens, and the removal of remaining tips. A catalogue describes records rather than reproducing all their evidence. The full correspondence and tribunal findings remain necessary for a detailed account of decisions and responsibility.

A letter written after a disaster is evidence of what a person wanted an authority to hear. It is not automatically evidence that every factual claim in the letter was correct. A tribunal is an attempt to establish findings within a defined process. It is not the same thing as the complete experience of a community. Reading these records together means keeping their differences visible.

## The work inside the landscape

Calling an event a landslide describes motion. It does not finish the explanation of how unstable material came to be there, who understood its condition, or who could require a change. The language of nature can be descriptively useful and politically incomplete at the same time.

The distinction is not a reason to replace geology with a moral slogan. Water, ground conditions and the behavior of accumulated material require careful technical explanation. But technical mechanisms acquire their relevance within decisions about siting, monitoring and continued use. A good account should not force readers to choose between physics and responsibility.

This is one reason maps matter. They can connect an administrative record to the space it describes: a tip, a settlement, routes of movement, a place where children learned. A map cannot supply the missing perspective of a resident, but it can prevent an institutional account from floating free of the people affected by its decisions.

## After the finding

An inquiry report can give a narrative a powerful ending. It gathers evidence, reaches conclusions and seems to transform uncertainty into order. For those living with the consequences, however, the publication of findings is another date in a much longer history.

The next questions concern implementation and remembrance. What was changed? Who paid? What did the community have to ask for again? Those questions need further records rather than a confident assertion that lessons were learned.

Aberfan demands a form of attention that resists both spectacle and premature closure. The archive should make authority traceable without making grief an illustration. It should also allow the reader to see that the hillside was not outside the world of decisions. Long before it moved, it had already been shaped by them.
