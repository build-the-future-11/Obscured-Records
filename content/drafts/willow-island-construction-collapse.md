On 27 April 1978, a cooling tower under construction at the Pleasants Power Station in Willow Island, West Virginia, collapsed inward. Fifty-one workers were on the scaffold attached to the rising shell. All of them died.

The catastrophe can be described in a few seconds: a hoisting cable went slack, a crane fell toward the interior, and concrete, forms and scaffolding followed. But that visible sequence began inside a construction system in which yesterday's concrete had to carry today's work.

The National Bureau of Standards, now the National Institute of Standards and Technology, joined the Occupational Safety and Health Administration's investigation. Its conclusion was carefully framed. The most probable cause was the imposition of construction loads on Lift 28 before its concrete had gained adequate strength to support them.

That finding makes Willow Island more than a story about a structure falling. It is a story about the temporary state of a structure becoming the workplace.

## A tower that carried its own construction

The second natural-draft cooling tower had reached about 166 feet. Its reinforced-concrete shell was being raised in successive lifts. A scaffold and formwork system moved upward with the work, while material was hoisted to the crew near the top.

The finished tower and the tower under construction were not the same load-bearing object. In service, a completed shell would act as a continuous structure with concrete that had developed its intended properties. During construction, the newest portion was young concrete connected to equipment, forms, scaffolds and workers. The construction method made the growing shell support the process that was creating it.

Concrete does not acquire its eventual strength the moment it is placed. The relevant question was therefore not simply whether the geometry had been designed to stand. It was whether the material in the newest lift was strong enough, at that time, for the loads actually applied.

NBS combined site inspection, laboratory testing and structural analysis. Its report located initiation in Lift 28 near cathead number 4. Calculated forces at several points there equaled or exceeded the shell's strength in compression, bending and shear. Once those points failed, the analysis found, failure could propagate around the lift.

## The previous day's concrete

NIST's event account says that, just after 10 a.m., a cable hoisting a bucket of concrete went slack and the crane fell inward. Lift 28, placed the previous day, began to collapse. The concrete shell peeled around the tower, carrying the wooden forms and metal scaffold into the hollow center.

The chronology matters because “concrete failure” can sound like a defect discovered in a mature structure. Here, the federal finding concerned the relation between developing strength and construction load. The material had not yet gained enough strength for what the construction system asked it to carry.

A later NBS analysis tested alternate conditions. It found that failure would initiate in Lift 28 at a concrete strength of 1,000 psi or less under the modeled conditions, and that 4,000 psi would have been needed for a safety factor of two. It also examined details such as an additional connection bolt and the location of the static-line ground anchor. Those results belong to the report's models; they should not be converted into a universal curing-time rule for other projects.

The stronger lesson is procedural. Age alone is not strength, and a schedule is not a measurement. When a temporary construction stage depends on a material property that changes over time, the decision to advance the work depends on evidence that the needed property exists.

## A failure with no refuge

The workers' exposure was concentrated on the same moving platform that depended on the shell. When the supporting construction stage failed, the workplace failed with it. There was no separate floor, stair tower or distant operating position in the event sequence described by NIST.

That does not, by itself, settle questions of legal responsibility or regulatory compliance. The NBS investigation was a structural failure study carried out at OSHA's request. Its engineering conclusion should not be stretched into unreviewed claims about who knew what, which rules applied, or what a court decided.

It does show why temporary works deserve the same analytical seriousness as permanent ones. Forms, scaffolds, hoists, anchors and recently placed material can create a load path that exists only during construction. It may disappear from the finished building, but workers occupy it while it exists.

Willow Island's final tower was never the object that failed. The object that failed was a partially completed shell carrying a live construction operation. Any account that looks only at the intended finished structure misses the condition that made the disaster possible.

## What the record supports

The public NIST record supports the date, toll, physical sequence and most-probable-cause finding. It also supports a specific initiating location and the conclusion that the young concrete's strength was inadequate for the applied construction loads.

The structural record is already exact enough to resist a vague label such as “construction accident.” At Willow Island, the construction sequence was part of the structure, the unfinished structure was the workplace, and the margin between one lift and the next was measured in material strength—not in the confidence of the schedule.
