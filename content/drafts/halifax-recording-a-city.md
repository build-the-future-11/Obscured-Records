A disaster does not leave behind one account. It leaves lists, letters, photographs, claims, official reports and recollections. Some are made to explain. Others are made because someone needs help, money, recognition or a record of a person who has disappeared.

The Halifax Explosion occurred on 6 December 1917 after a collision in the harbor involving the ammunition ship Mont-Blanc. Nova Scotia Archives brings together material documenting the event and its aftermath. The collection matters not only for the information each item contains, but for the different reasons those items came into existence.

A relief application and a personal letter may concern the same household while describing it in very different terms. One asks for information that an institution can process. The other may preserve details irrelevant to an eligibility rule but central to a life. Neither should be mistaken for a complete portrait.

### A list is made for a purpose

Names in disaster records are not simply found and assembled. They are collected through procedures. An investigator should ask when a list was compiled, which sources it used and what counted as inclusion. Changes across versions can reflect corrections, different definitions or information arriving later.

This does not mean that every count is arbitrary. It means that a count has a history. Showing that history can make a number more trustworthy than presenting it without explanation.

The same is true of photographs. A camera records a view from a position. The image may establish damage visible at that location, but it cannot establish the condition of an entire city or tell us what happened outside the frame. Captions need their own verification rather than inheriting authority from the photograph's age.

### Reading across the files

The richest reconstruction often comes from bringing documents into a relationship they did not originally have. A street address can connect an image to a relief record. A name can connect a family letter to an official account. The connection should be recorded carefully enough that another reader can follow it.

At the same time, a gap must remain a gap. Two similar names do not establish one identity. A plausible route through a damaged street is not evidence that a particular person took it. Archival writing becomes fiction when it silently fills those spaces to maintain narrative momentum.

Halifax can be remembered through the extraordinary force of the explosion. Its archive also asks for attention to the slower labor of accounting: locating people, describing needs, recording damage and preserving a city that could not be restored simply by rebuilding its structures.

The files do not bring that city back intact. They allow a more modest and more durable act: showing what a particular document can establish, what it leaves out, and how it belongs beside other surviving traces.
