The Halifax Explosion on 6 December 1917 left a casualty record that changed as documents were assembled and corrected. Nova Scotia Archives distinguishes a 1918 directory with 1,535 identified victims, an official register with 1,631 entries and a combined database with 1,782 names.

The totals belong to different records. The harbor collision involving the ammunition ship Mont-Blanc was one event; identifying everyone killed became a continuing undertaking. Registers, correspondence and corrections changed the record as more information reached the people compiling it.

A relief application and a personal letter may concern the same household while describing it in very different terms. One asks for information that an institution can process. The other may preserve details irrelevant to an eligibility rule but central to a life. Neither should be mistaken for a complete portrait.

### A list is made for a purpose

Names in disaster records are not simply found and assembled. They are collected through procedures. An investigator should ask when a list was compiled, which sources it used and what counted as inclusion. Changes across versions can reflect corrections, different definitions or information arriving later.

The same is true of photographs. A camera records a view from a position. The image may establish damage visible at that location, but it cannot establish the condition of an entire city or tell us what happened outside the frame. Captions need their own verification rather than inheriting authority from the photograph's age.

### The number and its sources

Nova Scotia Archives explains how its remembrance database was assembled and revised. Its account distinguishes a 1918 directory listing 1,535 identified victims, an official fatalities register containing 1,631 entries when it closed in December 1918, and a combined database listing 1,782 names. These are records produced through different procedures, not three interchangeable estimates.

The archive describes integrating the official register after its transfer in 2004, adding corrections and linking digitized pages to individuals. It also warns that some names may never be recovered. The database's apparent precision therefore coexists with an explicit limit on completeness.

A revised count can reflect another source, a corrected identity or a discrepancy resolved against archival material. Publishing the records behind the revision makes that change inspectable.

The database links numbers to named people and surviving documents. Its limit is equally visible: the archive warns that some names may never be recovered.

### Reading across the files

The richest reconstruction often comes from bringing documents into a relationship they did not originally have. A street address can connect an image to a relief record. A name can connect a family letter to an official account. The connection should be recorded carefully enough that another reader can follow it.

At the same time, a gap must remain a gap. Two similar names do not establish one identity. A plausible route through a damaged street is not evidence that a particular person took it. Archival writing becomes fiction when it silently fills those spaces to maintain narrative momentum.

The rebuilding of streets could not restore the details lost with their inhabitants. Registers, relief files and letters preserved different fragments. Connecting them carefully can recover a fuller record without pretending that the remaining gaps have disappeared.
