The 2003 fire at The Station nightclub in Rhode Island killed 100 people. NIST investigated how pyrotechnics ignited foam, how conditions inside the building deteriorated and how occupants tried to escape. The inquiry connects fire growth with the time and routes available for evacuation.

Escape depended on more than the number of doors. Occupants had to reach and pass through a usable route before heat, smoke and congestion closed that opportunity. The materials that fed the fire and the systems available to suppress it helped set the time limit.

## Two clocks in the same room

NIST's account records crowding at the main entrance and the absence of sprinklers. It also describes a mockup experiment in which an installed sprinkler system controlled the test fire. That experiment supplies evidence about suppression under its test conditions; it is not a filmed alternative outcome for every person in the actual nightclub. [NIST investigation, fire protection and emergency egress findings](https://www.nist.gov/disaster-failure-studies/station-nightclub-fire-2003).

The conceptual problem involves two clocks. One measures how long occupants need to escape. The other measures how long the environment remains survivable along the routes they must use. Counting doors addresses only part of the first clock. Materials and suppression affect the second. The clocks interact because worsening conditions can slow movement while simultaneously shortening the time left.

Crowding and smoke constrain movement even when people make reasonable choices. Attributing the outcome to panic bypasses the physical conditions the investigation examined.

## A route on paper is not an evacuation

An exit count is a static description. Escape is a process. People have to recognize danger, find a route, reach it and move through it while visibility, heat and available space may be changing. These steps can overlap, and the slowest constraint can govern the whole process.

An alternative door may look obvious on a retrospective floor plan. That view does not establish what an occupant could see or reach as conditions changed.

It also matters how an investigation establishes its timing. A video, a laboratory experiment and a computer model are different kinds of evidence. A model can test a mechanism under stated assumptions. It cannot become a recording of an event merely because its animation is convincing. Experimental reconstruction is most useful when the reader can see both what was reproduced and what remained uncertain.

## Before anyone needs to decide

Evacuation decisions take place within conditions established before the emergency: materials, layout, suppression and recognizable exits. Examining behavior requires examining those conditions too.

A venue's ordinary atmosphere is part of the challenge. People come to listen, watch and gather, not to conduct an inspection. Safety has to exist before visitors are required to understand every relevant hazard. The everyday use of a room should not demand expert knowledge as a condition of survival.

The exits and the fire cannot be assessed separately. One governed where people could go; the other governed how long they had to get there. That relationship, rather than a count of doors, is central to the investigation.
