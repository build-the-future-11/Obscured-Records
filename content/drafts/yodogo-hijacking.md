Nine members of the Red Army Faction diverted Japan Airlines Flight 351, a domestic flight from Tokyo to Fukuoka, and took refuge in North Korea in March 1970. Japan's National Police Agency identifies the incident as the Yodo-go hijacking.

The agency's 2019 history also recounts testimony given in 2002 about involvement in abductions. Those claims concern a later evidentiary stage. The aircraft's arrival in North Korea ended one part of the episode while leaving subsequent questions of jurisdiction and accountability unsettled.

## The event that crossed a border and stayed there

A hijacking is often narrated as a crisis with a clear end: the plane lands, passengers leave and authorities regain control. A politically motivated diversion can create a longer history in which questions of jurisdiction and accountability outlast the immediate flight.

The police history establishes the agency's retrospective account. Motives, later diplomacy and life after the hijacking require other records, rather than an expanded inference from its compact incident description.

An organizational affiliation can identify a group without explaining every individual's motives. The distinction also matters when similar names are used for different organizations.

Borders complicate the record as well as the response. Material may be preserved in different languages, under different access rules and by institutions with opposing interests. An absence from one archive does not establish that no relevant document exists elsewhere. Translation choices should be retained where they affect a disputed claim.

Immediate danger to passengers and later legal or diplomatic consequences have different endpoints. Their chronology must retain both.

## Reading the police account at its own date

Japan's National Police Agency included the hijacking in a 2019 historical account of extremist activity. Its retrospective supplies a compact incident description, but it is not a transcript of the entire journey or every negotiation. Nor should statements about investigations in that publication automatically be represented as the current position years later.

The Red Army Faction and the Japanese Red Army are not interchangeable labels. A later witness's testimony establishes that a claim was made; it is not automatically a judicial finding about everyone mentioned.

The flight's diversion was a defined event. The allegations concerning later activity required their own testimony and findings. The border crossing links those histories without making the evidence for one interchangeable with the evidence for the other.
