A steel tank holding roughly 2.2 million gallons of molasses ruptured in Boston’s North End. The contents surged through nearby streets, destroying structures, carrying debris and trapping people in a fluid that became harder to escape as it cooled.

Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. The tank had been built quickly, leaked from the beginning and was never subjected to the kind of full engineering scrutiny such a structure would now require.

## The material matters

The name of the disaster invites novelty. Molasses belongs to kitchens and food production, so its appearance in a lethal urban flood can sound like an absurd historical anecdote. That reaction is precisely what a serious account needs to move beyond.

A stored liquid has weight. A large volume released into streets can impose forces and create conditions that are entirely unlike its behavior in a jar. Familiarity with a household quantity is not an adequate intuition for an industrial inventory.

The investigation therefore has to follow the container as carefully as the contents. What was the tank designed to hold? What was its condition? What evidence supported confidence in it?
