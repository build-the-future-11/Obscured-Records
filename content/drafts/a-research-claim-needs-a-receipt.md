A polished research paragraph can arrive in seconds. A literature summary, a hypothesis, a script, a table and the prose around them can be generated in one smooth sequence. The speed is useful. It also makes it easy to mistake a finished-looking account for a checked one. A citation can be plausible and wrong; a script can run while answering the wrong question; a result can be real within one dataset and fragile outside it.

The remedy is an evidence trail: a record connecting each important claim to the source, data, decisions and computation that support it. A reader should be able to follow the path from sentence to source or from result to dataset and run, and see where that path ends. In a 2023 study of ChatGPT-generated bibliographies, researchers found both fabricated references and substantive errors in entries for real works. That is a reason to inspect citations at the source. It is also a reminder that a bibliography alone cannot tell us whether a source actually supports the sentence attached to it. [5]

## A paper, code and result make different promises

A paper is a report of claims. Code is a procedure that may generate outputs. A result is what a particular execution produced under stated conditions. Those pieces can support one another, but they should not be collapsed into a single badge of “verified.” The National Academies’ report on reproducibility and replicability draws a useful distinction: computational reproducibility means obtaining consistent results with the same input data, methods, code and conditions; replication tests a scientific question with new data. [1] Even among research communities, the terms can vary, which makes it all the more important to say what was actually checked.

The ACM’s artifact policy reflects the same separation by treating artifact availability, artifact functionality or reusability, and results reproduced or replicated as distinct outcomes. [2] A repository being public establishes availability. A reviewer or reader getting the code to run establishes a further fact. Neither, by itself, establishes that the data are appropriate, the evaluation is sound or the paper’s wider interpretation follows.

Machine-learning research gives concrete examples of why the steps matter. Kapoor and Narayanan’s cross-disciplinary study catalogued leakage problems across fields that use machine learning in science. In a civil-war prediction case study, correcting leakage erased the apparent substantive advantage of complex models over logistic regression. [4] The paper could look convincing and the computation could be made repeatable while the original comparison still overstated what the evidence showed.

That is why a useful record needs more than a link to code. It should identify data and model versions, preprocessing choices, train/validation/test splits, prompts or instructions, software and hardware conditions, exact commands, random seeds where relevant, and which outputs correspond to the claims in the paper. NeurIPS’s reproducibility checklist asks authors for code, data and instructions, including the exact command and environment; it also asks for training details, data splits, multiple-run uncertainty and compute information. Where materials cannot be released, the checklist allows authors to explain why and specify what can be reproduced. [3]

## A null result belongs in the record

An evidence trail must preserve the work that did not produce the headline. Failed runs can reveal a bug, a fragile assumption or a boundary on the claim. A null result can help distinguish “we did not detect an effect” from “the effect is absent,” especially when the estimate is imprecise. Leaving those attempts out can make a selectively successful path look inevitable.

This is not just a theoretical concern. In a study of 221 survey experiments with a known publication history, strong findings were substantially more likely than null findings to be written up and published. The authors traced the bias in that sample to researchers not writing up and submitting null results. [6] The finding should not be stretched into a universal rate for every discipline. It does show how an incomplete public record can tilt what later readers believe the evidence says.

AI can make the selection harder to see by quickly generating alternatives: new prompts, feature sets, analyses, model settings or explanations. For each reported result, authors should record how many materially different attempts were made, what was preplanned, what changed, and which estimates were not selected for the paper. The point is not to publish every temporary file. It is to preserve enough context to understand the selection process and uncertainty. For medical-journal submissions, ICMJE guidance makes human authors responsible for reviewing AI-generated material, checking its sources and disclosing how AI was used. [8]

## Keep the holdout meaningful

The same principle applies to test data. A holdout is evidence about performance only while it remains meaningfully separate from decisions made during development. Repeatedly checking it while choosing prompts, thresholds or model settings can tune a system to that set. Dwork and colleagues showed how adaptive reuse of a holdout can undermine the guarantees people expect from a test set. [7] In AI research, exposure may also come through training corpora or prior benchmark iterations, so evaluations should document what was held back, who had access, and how often results were consulted.

A practical evidence card can make the chain legible without turning every article into a lab notebook. For each central claim, give readers the cited source and the specific support it provides; the data and model versions; the code, command and environment needed to rerun the reported analysis; a concise log of key attempts and exclusions; and the limits on access or generalization. Mark what has only been described, what has run, what has been independently checked, and what remains uncertain.

Generative systems can make research prose and artifacts faster to assemble. Scientific confidence still depends on whether another person can inspect the route from claim to evidence, identify the decisions along the way and see what did not work. The receipt is part of the result.
