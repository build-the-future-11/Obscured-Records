The Silver Bridge investigation poses an uncomfortable question for any inspection program: what happens when the decisive defect is somewhere an inspector cannot see? The answer cannot simply be to look more carefully. It requires examining how the structure carries load, how its material can fail and whether the inspection method can reach the vulnerable location.

At approximately 5 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on December 15, 1967, the U.S. 35 bridge between Point Pleasant, West Virginia, and Kanauga, Ohio, collapsed. Forty-six people died and nine were injured. The National Transportation Safety Board traced the initiating failure to eyebar 330 at joint C13N in the north suspension chain on the Ohio side. [1](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/80267.aspx)

## A small fracture in a consequential place

An eyebar is a tension link with an enlarged, perforated end through which a connecting pin passes. The National Bureau of Standards' institutional history describes the bridge's suspension members as chains of paired eyebars rather than conventional wire cables. The bars used quenched and tempered steel with high yield strength, but the material had poor resistance to crack propagation. Ordinary tensile behavior had not revealed that vulnerability. [2](https://nistdigitalarchives.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/collection/p15421coll1/id/9820/download)

The distinction matters because strength and resistance to a growing crack are different properties. A design can efficiently carry its intended load while remaining vulnerable to a defect at a particular location. Describing the steel as strong does not answer how much damage it could tolerate. Nor does a small defect necessarily imply a small consequence; its position within the load-carrying system matters.

The NTSB found a brittle fracture in the lower part of the eyebar eye, followed by a ductile fracture above it. The adjoining eyebar then slipped from the joint pin, separating the chain. Investigators attributed the critical flaw's growth to stress corrosion and corrosion fatigue. They found that its location prevented visual detection and that available inspection methods would have required dismantling the joint. That last finding describes the investigation's technological context, not the limits of all inspection equipment today. [1](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/80267.aspx)

## Reconstructing which break came first

The official NBS history records metallurgist John Bennett examining the wreckage in January 1968. One side of the broken eye showed a flat fracture; the other showed tearing and deformation. Those different surfaces helped identify the initiating failure rather than damage produced during the collapse. Later laboratory work, including full-scale eyebar testing at Battelle, examined whether the observed pre-existing crack was large enough to trigger brittle fracture. [2](https://nistdigitalarchives.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/collection/p15421coll1/id/9820/download)

This distinction is central to failure investigation. A collapsed bridge supplies many damaged parts, but their presence alone does not establish the sequence. Investigators need evidence that discriminates between a component that started the collapse and one broken by it. The archive is particularly valuable here because it records how a plausible early interpretation was subjected to further testing rather than being treated as sufficient by itself.

## Making inspection a national responsibility

FHWA's history connects the disaster with Section 26 of the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1968, signed on August 23. It directed development of national inspection standards covering methods, maximum intervals and inspector qualifications. States were to retain reports and record action taken on findings; a training program was also required. [3](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/highwayhistory/national_bridge_inspection_standards.cfm)

The first National Bridge Inspection Standards regulation followed on April 27, 1971. FHWA describes its requirement for trained inspectors, inspection at least every two years, written findings and follow-up needs. Inspection results also supplied a national bridge inventory. These are features of the original program; the two-year interval should not be read as a complete statement of today's rules. [3](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/highwayhistory/national_bridge_inspection_standards.cfm)

Seen analytically, the reform joined observation to a durable administrative record. A report can preserve a deterioration trend across personnel changes. Recording the response makes it possible to distinguish a defect that was noticed from one that was addressed. An inventory makes the individual inspection part of a wider population of structures. None of those functions makes an inaccessible flaw visible, but each supports decisions about what requires closer investigation.

## Naming the structural vulnerability

The 2022 federal rule replaced the term fracture-critical member with nonredundant steel tension member. Its definition concerns a primary steel component under tension whose failure could bring down part or all of a bridge because specified forms of redundancy are absent. It defines the corresponding inspection as hands-on. The rule also discusses why the older terminology could mislead people unfamiliar with the standards. [4](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-05-06/pdf/2022-09512.pdf)

The useful question is therefore conditional: what follows if this member fails? It is not a prediction that any bridge carrying the designation will collapse. Silver Bridge's record supports examining detectability and consequences together. Inspection frequency, physical access, material behavior and structural alternatives address different parts of that question; none can safely stand in for all the others.
