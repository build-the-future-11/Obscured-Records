Typhoon Nina brought extreme rainfall to Henan province in August 1975, overwhelming a region containing multiple dams and downstream settlements. Banqiao and Shimantan were among the dams that failed.

Long Yang and colleagues' 2017 study reconstructs rainfall over 5–7 August from observations and a weather model. It links atmospheric blocking to the storm's slow movement and sustained rain. That explains the meteorological conditions; engineering and response records must establish what happened as the dams and downstream communities confronted them.

## One number cannot contain a cascade

A dam disaster can be described through a breached structure, but the consequences may spread through a system of reservoirs, settlements and services. The first failure and the final toll are connected by many events that deserve to remain visible.

The hydrometeorological study addresses the rainfall and flooding, not every decision made by dam operators or emergency authorities. Its detailed treatment of weather cannot, on its own, establish the full institutional history.

Mortality estimates need particular care. Deaths during flooding, deaths associated with subsequent illness and other indirect losses may be counted over different periods. A large combined estimate should not be presented as if it were a count made at a single location immediately after a breach.

A system perspective also changes the meaning of protection. A structure can be designed against a particular condition while the wider network remains vulnerable to an event outside that assumption. Warnings, evacuation routes and communications can affect consequences independently of the structural behavior. Their role should be examined rather than inferred from the outcome.

The sequence between rainfall, reservoir inflows, structural failures and downstream harm is the central problem. Neither the size of the storm nor a single failed structure can account for every link in that chain.

## Rainfall has a geography

Yang and colleagues examine the rainfall over 5–7 August, distinguishing changes in the storm's organization across those days. Their analysis uses observations and a weather model. They describe atmospheric blocking as important to the storm's slow movement and prolonged rainfall.

A reservoir receives runoff from a catchment over time. Similar rainfall totals can produce different demands when rain falls in different places or at different rates.

A model adds a further distinction. It is an analytical reconstruction whose results are compared with observations, not a new observation made in 1975. The reconstruction can help explain a mechanism while remaining dependent on its data, assumptions and resolution.

Meteorological reconstruction cannot decide whether a design assumption was adequate or a warning arrived in time. Those questions require engineering and response records.

Rainfall reconstruction establishes what the storm delivered. Engineering records must show how the structures responded, and response records how warnings and assistance moved downstream. Banqiao's history depends on connecting those accounts without treating any one as the whole explanation.
