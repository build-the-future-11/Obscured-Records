On 2 April 2010, workers at the Tesoro refinery in Anacortes, Washington, were switching a process stream between two banks of heat exchangers. One exchanger ruptured. The resulting explosion and fire fatally injured seven employees.

The [U.S. Chemical Safety Board’s investigation](https://www.csb.gov/tesoro-anacortes-refinery-fatal-explosion-and-fire-/) described the failed exchanger as nearly forty years old. Its age matters, but age alone is not an explanation. The Board traced the rupture to high-temperature hydrogen attack, a damage mechanism that can create microscopic fissures inside carbon steel exposed to hydrogen at elevated temperature and pressure.

This is a story about damage that accumulated before it became visible as an emergency. It is also a story about the limits of a safety system built around what its rules and inspections expect to find.

## A routine operation met a hidden condition

The flow switch was an operating task, not an emergency response. Workers were positioned near equipment that had performed refinery service for decades. The event became catastrophic when the exchanger shell could no longer contain the process material.

According to the [CSB final investigation report](https://www.csb.gov/file.aspx?DocumentId=5851), high-temperature hydrogen attack had cracked and weakened the carbon steel. Once the exchanger ruptured, hot, flammable material escaped and ignited. The seven people killed were close to the equipment because the plant was carrying out the switching operation around it.

That sequence changes the central question. The rupture was not simply a sudden break in old machinery. The report asks why a known damage mechanism was not identified and controlled before workers had to stand within its consequence zone.

## A boundary on a chart is not a physical barrier

Refinery operators use industry guidance to judge when steel equipment may be susceptible to hydrogen damage. Such guidance can organize experience, but it is not the material itself. The CSB concluded that equipment had failed under conditions treated by the prevailing industry method as outside the expected danger region.

That mismatch has two implications. First, an inspection strategy can be internally consistent and still miss the mechanism that matters. Second, operating history must be allowed to challenge the assumptions embedded in a standard. When evidence of failure appears beyond an accepted boundary, treating the boundary as fixed preserves the model at the expense of the plant.

The investigation also examined the choice of material. More resistant alloys were available for this service. A publication-ready account must distinguish what the report established from the broader counterfactual: it can report the Board’s materials finding, but it cannot claim that any single substitution would have eliminated every risk without examining design, fabrication and operating conditions together.

## Inspection must be designed for the damage

Mechanical-integrity programs often produce large amounts of activity: scheduled checks, thickness readings, work orders and signoffs. Tesoro shows why the existence of inspection is different from the ability to detect a particular form of deterioration.

High-temperature hydrogen attack can develop internally. A program centered on more familiar wall-thinning mechanisms may not reveal it in time. The useful measure is therefore not how many inspections occurred, but whether the inspection method, location and interval could find the damage that the service conditions made plausible.

The CSB issued recommendations extending beyond the failed exchanger, including changes to industry guidance and regulatory practice. Those recommendations should be reported as responses to the investigation, not as proof that the hazard has been permanently resolved. Their implementation status requires a fresh check before publication.

## The workers belong at the center of the record

Technical language can make this accident sound like a contest between a curve, an alloy and a crack. Seven workers absorbed the consequence of every earlier assumption. Their proximity was not incidental; the task brought them beside equipment whose condition had not been understood.

A finished article needs reporting beyond the federal investigation. It should seek the experience of workers and families, the company’s response, the relevant union record, Washington’s regulatory actions and an independent metallurgical assessment. It should also determine how warnings, leaks, prior inspection findings and replacement decisions moved through the organization.

The public record already supports a narrower conclusion. A routine operation exposed workers to equipment carrying damage that the prevailing system had not controlled. The rupture made that damage visible all at once. The reporting task is to reconstruct how it remained invisible for so long.
