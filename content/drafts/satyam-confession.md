False invoices could make Satyam Computer Services appear to earn revenue; forged bank information could make the resulting cash appear to exist. The SEC's April 2011 announcement alleged that revenue, income and cash had been overstated by more than $1 billion over five years.

Satyam settled the SEC charges without admitting or denying the allegations. The agreement included a penalty and requirements concerning controls. These recorded obligations followed the alleged misstatements; evidence of their implementation and effectiveness belongs to the subsequent history.

## The cash balance as a claim

Cash appears reassuring because it sounds concrete. A reported balance suggests money somewhere that can be counted and confirmed. Yet the figure in a financial statement remains a claim until the evidence connecting it to an actual account has been examined.

An admission and an accounting reconstruction answer different questions. The amount, duration and mechanism of a misstatement still need records, even when a statement appears to offer a complete explanation.

The 2011 announcement reports allegations and settlement terms. Those procedural labels limit what it establishes about the underlying accounts.

There is also a difference between explaining a deception and explaining why it persisted. The first may involve entries, confirmations and transactions. The second concerns the institutions expected to challenge those representations. Neither question should be answered solely through the personality of the executive at the center of the story.

A reported balance depends on the statement asserting it, the documents supporting it and independent evidence from the account purportedly holding the money. Agreement among unreliable internal records is insufficient.

## The route from invoices to apparent cash

The SEC described fictitious invoices and falsified bank information in its 2011 action. These are distinct points in an accounting story. An invoice can create the appearance of revenue; a bank record can appear to confirm the resulting money. When both are unreliable, reconciliation within the company's own records may produce consistency without producing truth.

The settlement also belongs to a different phase from the misconduct described in the complaint. Satyam's new leadership agreed to controls-related measures and a monetary penalty without admitting or denying the allegations. Those terms establish what the company agreed to do, rather than proving that every subsequent safeguard worked. An account of institutional repair needs evidence after implementation. Treating the settlement as a finished solution would repeat the mistake of accepting a documented intention as a verified result.

The alleged scheme made invented revenue appear to produce real cash. The settlement prescribed a response. Establishing whether the new controls worked requires evidence from after their implementation, just as establishing the original misstatements requires the records behind the accounts.
