The Eastland capsized beside a Chicago River dock on 24 July 1915 while loaded for a Western Electric excursion. The Encyclopedia of Chicago reports 844 deaths. The vessel had scarcely begun its journey, and proximity to the city offered little protection to passengers unable to get out.

The disaster occurred beside a dock, not far out at sea. Distance from shore could not substitute for stability or a way out of enclosed spaces. Those protections depended on the vessel's design, loading and usable routes of escape.

## Safety depends on the whole vessel

Being close to shore can create a powerful impression of safety. It does not remove the need for stability, usable exits or a way out of an enclosed space. The Eastland's location is part of what makes the loss so difficult to understand through ordinary intuitions about maritime danger.

This does not mean that safety rules are inherently counterproductive. It means that a vessel must be evaluated as an arrangement whose parts interact, rather than as a checklist of independently reassuring features.

## Changes to a whole vessel

The Encyclopedia of Chicago describes alterations affecting Eastland's stability before the disaster, as well as additional lifeboats and rafts in 1915. This history makes a simple explanation based on one safety measure inadequate. A ship's stability depends on the relationship among its hull, loading, weight distribution, and operation. An equipment change can matter within that relationship without being the sole cause of a capsizing.
