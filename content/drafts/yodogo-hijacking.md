Nine members of the Japanese Red Army Faction seized a Japan Airlines Boeing 727 on a domestic flight, armed with swords and a homemade bomb. They demanded to be flown to North Korea—a destination the crew had neither maps nor experience to reach.

After stops in Fukuoka and Seoul, and a hostage exchange involving a Japanese official, the aircraft reached Pyongyang. The hijackers stayed. Decades later, their presence remained entangled with allegations of abduction, ideological regret and diplomatic stalemate.

## The event that crossed a border and stayed there

A hijacking is often narrated as a crisis with a clear end: the plane lands, passengers leave and authorities regain control. A politically motivated diversion can create a longer history in which questions of jurisdiction and accountability outlast the immediate flight.

The Japanese National Police Agency material is an official account with a specific institutional purpose. It can establish what the agency says about the event and the people it identifies. A broader political history requires additional sources, especially when it describes motives, life after the hijacking or later diplomatic negotiations.

Borders complicate the record as well as the response. Material may be preserved in different languages, under different access rules and by institutions with opposing interests. An absence from one archive does not establish that no relevant document exists elsewhere. Translation choices should be retained where they affect a disputed claim.

For readers, the useful chronology has more than one endpoint. It separates the end of immediate danger to passengers from the subsequent legal and diplomatic history. Doing so avoids suggesting that landing the aircraft settled everything that had begun aboard it. The flight can end while the questions created by it remain open.

The episode is more than an eccentric hijacking. It captures the point where domestic student radicalism crossed borders and hardened into an international network, leaving families and governments to negotiate with the consequences for generations.
