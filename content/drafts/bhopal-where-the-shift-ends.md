The end of a shift is supposed to mark a boundary. A worker leaves a site and returns to ordinary life. An industrial release can erase that distinction, reaching the streets and homes around a plant and making the surrounding city part of the emergency.

A gas release at Bhopal in December 1984 caused mass death and injury. The International Labour Organization's 2004 account places the disaster within the history of occupational safety and the consequences of major industrial accidents. It also recognizes that the effects continued beyond the first hours. This draft does not select a single definitive lifetime casualty total from estimates produced for different purposes.

That restraint is not a way of making the event smaller. A number means something only when the reader knows what it counts: immediate deaths, later deaths attributed to exposure, recorded injuries or a defined population under study. Adding unlike totals together does not produce a more respectful account.

## More than one population at risk

Workplace safety and environmental safety can be administered separately. An accident does not have to respect the distinction. Employees, nearby residents, responders and people arriving later may face different forms of exposure, different access to information and different routes to assistance.

The geography matters. Distance from a fence is not necessarily an adequate description of exposure. Nor does being outside the workforce make someone's need for information secondary. Planning has to consider how a hazard could travel and who would have to make decisions before a complete explanation became available.

An emergency also creates a demand for knowledge that is practical before it is comprehensive. Responders need usable information about the substance and the circumstances. Residents need instructions they can understand and act on. Later scientific investigation remains essential, but it cannot substitute for communication during the period when people are deciding what to do.

## The time after the emergency

A disaster's administrative history can move faster than its human consequences. A case may be closed, a payment made or an institution reorganized while people continue to seek treatment, documentation or answers. The existence of one milestone does not establish the completion of all the others.

Conversely, a claim about a continuing effect needs evidence appropriate to that claim. The archive should distinguish testimony about experience, medical findings, exposure studies and legal decisions. It should not force them into a single scale of certainty or treat one as a replacement for the rest.

Bhopal is often invoked as a warning from the past. The more difficult task is to describe the responsibilities that persist after attention moves elsewhere. A factory can stop operating. The people around it cannot stop having a history with the site simply because an institutional timeline has reached its final page.
