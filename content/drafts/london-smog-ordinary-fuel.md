The frightening feature of a pollution disaster is sometimes the familiarity of its ingredients. A household burns fuel to keep warm. A factory produces smoke. A city continues to function. Then the atmosphere stops dispersing what people have come to expect it to carry away.

The Met Office describes London's Great Smog from 5 to 9 December 1952 as a convergence of cold weather, coal burning and conditions that trapped pollution close to the ground. Its account explains the temperature inversion that prevented the usual mixing. Thousands died. The precise mortality estimate requires attention to the period and method being counted; the violence of the event does not depend on choosing the largest number available.

### A disaster without a single point of release

An explosion gives an investigator a time and a location to begin with. Distributed pollution can be harder to narrate. The harmful material may emerge from many ordinary activities, and no single chimney supplies a complete explanation.

That makes the relationship between weather and emissions central. Weather can explain why smoke remained concentrated, but it does not explain why that particular mixture was being emitted or what alternatives people had. Equally, an account of fuel policy cannot replace the physical explanation of how exposure developed. The two histories belong together.

It would be too easy to call everyone responsible because many people burned coal. Responsibility is not distributed simply by counting chimneys. Households' choices depend on income, housing and access to fuel. Institutions have different capacities to alter infrastructure and set conditions. An analysis that treats every source of smoke as an equal exercise of power loses those differences.

### What becomes visible afterward

A photograph can show that a street was obscured. It cannot show all the exposure that followed someone indoors, nor can it establish which later death should be attributed to the episode. The image and the mortality analysis answer different questions.

This creates an editorial obligation to explain numbers without pretending that uncertainty makes the disaster unreal. A count can change because investigators use a longer follow-up period or a different comparison, not because somebody has discovered a hidden pile of bodies. The method is part of the meaning of the estimate.

The smog also challenges the idea that an event ends when the view clears. Dispersal removes an immediate atmospheric condition. It does not reverse an injury or guarantee that the same conjunction cannot recur. An account concerned with prevention has to follow the ordinary systems beyond the extraordinary days.

London's darkened streets remain a striking historical image. The harder thing to picture is the arrangement that made the smoke ordinary before it became catastrophic. That arrangement is where the longer story begins.
